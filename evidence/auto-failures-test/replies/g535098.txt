I have not built a rubric, and I think that is the right call.

You told me two things. Nothing has gone wrong with the quiz round names, and a bad name costs you nothing as far as you can tell. A rubric earns its keep when a missed failure has a real price. Without one, a fixed checklist is extra work that you would stop filling in after a week. This file says the same: if the task has no real stakes, a rubric is unnecessary overhead.

There is a second problem. You have no bad example, so I would be guessing at what "bad" means. Anything I wrote would be invented, not based on what you have seen.

What I would do instead is the following.

- Keep picking names the way you do now. "Name That Tune, Nearly" and "Capital Ideas" are good, and your own ear is a fine judge for this.
- If a name ever lands badly, write down what went wrong. Say the team found one confusing, or it gave away an answer, or it was a poor fit for the people in the room. That note is your first bad example.
- Come back with that example and I will build a rubric from it. Two or three real misses would be enough to say what a good name looks like.

One case would change my answer. If these names go outside the team, for instance to clients, or into something with a brand or a prize attached, a bad name could cost something. Tell me that and we can look again.
