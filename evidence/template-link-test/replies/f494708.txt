I'm not going to build a rubric for this one, and the reason comes from your own answers.

The instructions I was given say to stop when the task has no real stakes if it goes wrong. You told me a bad intro costs "nothing much", and that nothing has gone wrong so far. A two-line newsletter intro fits that. A fixed scoring scale, a list of automatic failures and a record sheet would take longer to fill in than the intro takes to read. They would add work without catching anything that matters.

You also couldn't give me a bad example. A rubric built without one would be guesswork about what might go wrong, not a check based on something that has.

What to do instead:

- Read the two lines before the newsletter goes out. That is the whole check.
- If you want a standing instruction for the AI, your two good examples already show the style. Something like: "Write a friendly two-line intro for our monthly office newsletter, in the style of these examples. No jokes about named people, no news that isn't in the issue."
- If an intro ever does go wrong (it names someone it shouldn't, hints at news that isn't in the issue, or gets the tone wrong for a sensitive month), save it. That becomes your first bad example, and you can come back and run this again with something real to work from.

The same would change if the AI started writing something with higher stakes, such as announcements about redundancies or policy changes, or anything sent outside the office. Then a rubric would be worth the effort.
